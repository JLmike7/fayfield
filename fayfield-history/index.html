Fayfield History

Before these streets were streets, they were a runway.

Fayfield sits in Springettsbury Township, York County, Pennsylvania, on ground that was York County’s first commercial airport. The turf field closed. Lots were auctioned. After the war, a subdivision named Fayfield filled the grid. This page is a researched neighborhood story, not an official history and not a complete archive. Where we are unsure, we say so.

The airfield (1930–1937)

In 1930 the York Chamber of Commerce organized York Airport, Inc. on about 157 acres west of what was then Airport Road — today’s Haines Road — between what are now 7th and 9th Avenues.

Scheduled passenger flights to Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, and New York began on October 15, 1930. The official dedication was October 25–26. A ticket to Philadelphia cost $8. The field had turf runways, hangars, and an office building with a ticket counter, waiting room, and restaurant. Local historian Stephen H. Smith writes that the core of that office still stands inside the house at 710 Haines Road (southwest corner of Haines and 7th).

The airport ran into money trouble. In July 1937 a plan to sell the land as lots was recorded. An auction of “50 Choice Homesites, Fronting on Haines Road at the York Airport” was advertised for July 24, 1937. That ad is also one of the earliest public uses of the name Haines Road instead of Airport Road.

Fayfield (1947–1950s)

Most of those Haines Road lots sat empty until after World War II. In 1947 the full Fayfield subdivision was approved, under the real estate firm Morgan, Zeigler and Philips, Inc., of 114 East Market Street, York. A May 24, 1947 notice in The Gazette and Daily announced the founding of Fayfield.

A 1949 newspaper aerial, as reported by Smith, said 173 of 415 lots were sold and 34 houses were up. The building boom came in the early 1950s. By 1955 most lots were built except those along the still-planned four-lane expressway. I-83 opened on October 28, 1959 and cut off an earlier idea: 7th and 9th Avenues running west to Mill Creek, with a bridge into Spring Garden Township.

Across Haines Road, Mahlon Haines sold his 205-acre Haines Acres farm for development in 1955.

Who could live here

The 1947 Fayfield subdivision plan included racially restrictive occupancy language. Jim McClure, writing in the York Daily Record in 2019, quotes that clause in a larger essay on redlining and housing in York County. We are not reprinting it here. The newspaper article is the public source.

Those restrictions cannot be enforced. In 1948 the U.S. Supreme Court held that courts may not enforce racially restrictive covenants. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 made racial occupancy rules unlawful. Some old plans and deeds in the county still contain the words; they are unenforceable.

This site is unofficial and resident-run. It does not speak for the township, an HOA, or the original developers.

Today

Fayfield remains a neighborhood in Springettsbury Township, not a borough and not part of the City of York. The township’s Fayfield Park is at 5th Avenue and Keesey Street (about 5 acres). Township history places Fayfield in the York Suburban School District, with East York, Haines Acres, and Yorkshire. The National Register East York Historic District is a different neighborhood, along East Market Street.

Uncertainties

  • The name Fayfield is speculated to come from “Fay” as a relative of a developer. Smith labels that speculation. We do not treat it as fact.
  • Amelia Earhart’s lecture in York on November 4, 1933 is in the newspaper. A landing at this airport is a family story Smith could not confirm in the press.
  • We have not yet read the 1947 plan ourselves in the county recorder’s office. McClure is our published source for the occupancy language.
  • We are not hosting historic photographs or maps. Rights sit with newspapers, the York County History Center, Penn Pilot, and photographers.
